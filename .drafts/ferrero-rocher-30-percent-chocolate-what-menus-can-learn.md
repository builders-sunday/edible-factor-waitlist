Every so often somebody turns a box of Ferrero Rocher around, reads the back, and posts a photo. The comments call it an exposé.

The label says each praline is 30% milk chocolate and 28.5% hazelnuts. The rest is sugar, palm oil, wheat flour, whey powder, fat-reduced cocoa, lecithin, sodium bicarbonate, salt and vanillin. Per 100 grams: 39.9 grams of sugar, 42.7 grams of fat.

None of that was hidden. It was printed on the box, in every shop that sold it.

And 30% is not a scandal. Ferrero Rocher is a filled praline, not a solid bar. Codex standards ask for at least 25% chocolate by weight in a praline. Ferrero declares 30%. It clears the bar, with real cocoa butter and cocoa mass.

The fair complaint is narrower, and it is about the wrapper. Gold foil, a pleated case and forty years of advertising imply something the ingredient list never promised. Nobody lied. The packaging is just better at suggesting quality than the recipe is at delivering it.

Now turn over a restaurant menu. A chocolate hazelnut praline at 420 rupees. There is nothing on the back. No percentage, no sugar figure, no calorie count, and no allergen line, on a dessert that almost certainly carries hazelnut, wheat and dairy.

Ferrero was accused of hiding a number it had printed. Most menus have never printed it at all.

Here is the part restaurant owners get backwards. Ferrero survived its viral week precisely because every number used against it came off its own box. It could answer in one sentence. A kitchen with no published numbers cannot make that reply, so it inherits whatever number the loudest commenter invents.

Disclosure is not a confession. It is the sentence you get to say when somebody asks.

There is a commercial edge too. Guests who avoid dairy or gluten or nuts do not negotiate at the table. They quietly pick somewhere else, and you never find out.

Ferrero's real advantage was never the foil. It was that when somebody finally turned the box around, the box had an answer.

Every so often somebody turns a box of Ferrero Rocher around, reads the back of it, and posts a photo. The comments treat it as an exposé.

Here is what is actually on the back. According to Ferrero's own UK ingredient list, each praline contains 30% milk chocolate and 28.5% hazelnuts. The rest of the list is sugar, palm oil, wheat flour, whey powder, fat-reduced cocoa, lecithin, sodium bicarbonate, salt and vanillin. The UK nutrition panel reports 39.9 grams of sugar and 42.7 grams of fat per 100 grams. Ferrero's American three-piece pack contains 15 grams of sugar and 230 calories, which works out to roughly 5 grams of sugar and 77 calories per golden ball.

Nothing there was leaked. Every number came off the packaging, where it had been sitting in plain sight in every shop that stocked the box.

That is the detail worth stealing if you run a restaurant.

## Thirty percent is a recipe, not a crime

Ferrero Rocher is a filled, layered praline, not a solid chocolate bar. The 30% describes the milk chocolate component of the finished sphere. The rest of the weight builds the wafer shell, the filling and the whole hazelnut in the middle. International Codex standards for chocolate and chocolate products say a praline should contain at least 25% chocolate by total weight. Ferrero declares 30%, so it clears the standard.

It is also real chocolate. The milk chocolate in it is made with cocoa butter and cocoa mass. Fake chocolate is the wrong charge, and making it let Ferrero off the hook for the thing it is actually guilty of.

## The fair complaint is about the wrapper

The honest criticism is narrower. Gold foil, a pleated paper case, a clear gift dome and decades of advertising set an expectation that the ingredient list never promised to meet. Nobody lied. The packaging is simply much better at implying quality than the recipe is at delivering it. That is a marketing question, not a food safety one, and the two get confused constantly.

## The frightening words, translated

Palm oil is on the list, and it sits in the filling rather than inside the declared milk chocolate component. It is used because it makes a smooth texture, stays stable and costs less than cocoa butter. It is also high in saturated fat, and the World Health Organization recommends keeping saturated fat below 10% of total energy intake. Ferrero says it sources 100% RSPO-certified palm oil, primarily through a segregated supply chain.

Vanillin is a flavouring compound used to reproduce the main flavour associated with vanilla. The American ingredient list describes it as an artificial flavour. Lecithin helps ingredients combine. Sodium bicarbonate helps create the wafer. Read slowly, the terrifying half of that list turns out to be a wafer recipe.

## Now run the same audit on a restaurant dessert

Say a restaurant lists a chocolate hazelnut praline at 420 rupees. Turn the menu over. There is nothing on the back.

No percentage. No sugar figure. No calorie count. No allergen line, even though a dessert built the way that Ferrero praline is built would carry hazelnut, wheat and dairy, three of the most common things a guest needs to avoid. A diner who wants to know asks the server, the server asks the kitchen, and the answer comes back as a confident guess.

This is the ordinary condition of eating out in India, and it is a bigger gap than the one the internet just got angry about. Ferrero was accused of hiding something it had printed. Most menus have genuinely never printed it.

## Disclosure is armour, not exposure

Restaurant owners tend to read a story like this as a warning to say less. It is the opposite lesson.

The only reason Ferrero came out of its viral week intact is that every number used against it came from Ferrero's own box. The company could answer in one sentence: that is on the label, and it clears the Codex minimum. A business with no published numbers cannot make that reply. It gets whatever number the loudest person in the comments invents, and then it argues from behind.

Publishing calories, allergens and price is not a confession. It is the sentence you get to say when somebody asks.

There is a quieter commercial point too. Guests who already avoid dairy or gluten or nuts do not negotiate at the table. They pick a different restaurant, before they leave the house, without telling you. Every unpublished allergen line is a booking that never happens and never gets counted.

## What this does not fix

Worth being straight about the limits. A published number does not change a recipe. It will not tell a diner whose palm oil is in the filling, whether the cocoa was traded fairly, or what a commodity price did last quarter. Those are supply chain problems, and menu transparency does not touch them.

What it does is close the gap between what a dish looks like and what a dish is. That is the exact gap the gold wrapper was working.
