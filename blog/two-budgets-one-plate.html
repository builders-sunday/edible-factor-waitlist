Every Meal Has Two Prices

A meal moves your calories and your rupees at the same moment. Most tools count one of them, so you end up doing the other one in your head.

By Nikhil Ballal

The 4 minute version

Calorie and food budget tracking is the idea behind Edible Factor: a meal costs you calories and rupees in the same moment, so one screen should count both. Every food tool I used before asked me to pick a side. Track what my body wants, or track what my wallet allows.

I never once ate a meal that only had one of those prices.

So we built the screen that shows both, and this post is about why, what it does in the demo, and what we are deliberately not claiming.

The body and the bank account still do not talk to each other. They now share a screen.

Why track calories and food spending together?

You should track calories and food spending together because you decide them together. Nobody stands at a counter and chooses a dish on calories, then walks away and chooses it again on price. It is one decision, made once, with two costs attached.

When those costs live in two places, one of them gets ignored. Usually the one that is less fun to look at, which changes depending on the day of the month.

What does a meal actually cost you?

A meal costs you a share of the day's energy and a share of the day's money, and both shares are gone the moment you eat it. That is the whole insight. It is not deep. It is just usually split across a calorie app, a banking app and a mental note that never gets written down.

The split is the problem I care about. Not the counting. Counting is easy. Holding two running totals in your head at lunch is not.

What happens on the screen when you save a meal?

When you save a meal, both counters drop together. In the demo, the day starts at 2,000 kcal and ₹500. Save a meal and they read 1,650 kcal and ₹410.

The demo meal is 350 kcal and ₹90. My maths, so you can check the screen is not lying: 2,000 minus 350 is 1,650, and ₹500 minus ₹90 is ₹410. These are demo screen values, not anyone's real day and not user data.

The point of the demo is the "together". One action, two numbers, no second app.

How many steps does it take to log a meal?

Logging a meal takes three steps: log it, see the cost, see the calories. That order is on purpose. Logging is the only thing you have to do. The other two are things the screen shows you back.

Every extra step in a logging flow is a day somebody stops logging. I would rather have three honest steps than a beautiful form nobody finishes.

What does "what fits" mean on the home screen?

"What fits" means the home screen counts how many dishes on our menus still fit what you have left today, in calories and in rupees at the same time. Set a daily budget, eat something, and the count changes.

I am not going to quote you a count here. It depends on your budget, on what you have already eaten and on which menus are live that day. A count that changes with every person and every lunch is not something I can honestly print here.

Is Edible Factor the only app that tracks calories and rupees?

I do not know, and I am not going to say it is. Other apps now score dishes for health. Good.

What I can say is narrower. We built Edible Factor around the pair, calories and price on the same dish and the same screen, because that is the decision people actually make.

Where do the calorie numbers come from?

The calorie numbers come from the restaurant, and they show where the restaurant has published them. The price comes from the restaurant too. When a number is missing, it should look missing, not quietly turn into a zero that makes a dish look free of consequences.

That is the same honesty rule I keep coming back to on this blog. A menu that quietly changes under you, like the one in the sugar price post, is a menu you cannot plan around. The same goes for a nutrition table nobody can trace, which is why cheese vs paneer names a source in every column. If you want the plumbing side of building this, I wrote about moving off Supabase on my own site.

How should you use two budgets?

  • Pick a daily rupee number you would actually keep, not an aspirational one.
  • Decide before you open the menu, not after the dish is already on its way.
  • Read the price and the calories on the same line. If one of them is missing, treat that as information.
  • Treat any demo number, including the ones in this post, as a demo.
  • Let a good meal blow one budget sometimes. A budget you never break is a budget set too loose.

What is this not?

This is not a diet plan and it is not financial advice. It is a counter. It tells you where you stand after a meal, in two units, so the next decision is a little less of a guess.

A meal is one decision with two prices. It deserves one screen.
