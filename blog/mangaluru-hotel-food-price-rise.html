Mangaluru's Plate May Cost More. Blame the Onion, Mostly

Mangaluru's hotels say onion, rice, dal and milk now cost far more, and estimate menus may rise 5 to 10%. Nothing has been decided, so what you pay depends on which hotel you walk into.

By Nikhil Ballal

The 5 minute version

The Mangaluru hotel food price hike is an estimate, not a decision. The Dakshina Kannada Hotels and Restaurants Association (DKHRA) says hotel food may cost 5 to 10% more, because the ingredients have jumped. Its secretary, Devi Prasad, says the rise will depend on the establishment, and there will be no uniform revision.

The kitchen numbers are the association's own. Onion went from about ₹20 a kg to ₹50 to ₹53, and has stayed there for nearly three months. Rice went from ₹50 to ₹52 a kg to about ₹85. Toor and urad dal went from ₹120 to ₹130 a kg to about ₹150. Then Nandini milk went up ₹8 a litre from 1 November.

So this is not really a menu story yet. It is a grocery bill story that has not reached the menu.

What has the Mangaluru hotels association actually said?

The DKHRA has said hotel food may cost 5 to 10% more overall, depending on the establishment. That is the secretary's range, reported by Asianet Newsable and Coastal Digest on 2 November 2026.

The association's president, Kudpi Jagadish Shenoy, put it at about 10% at some hotels. The secretary was careful about one thing: there will be no common increase. Each hotel will set its own rate.

That is three statements, easy to blur: an estimate overall, a guess for some hotels, and no common rate.

Why are Mangaluru hotel food prices going up?

Because a hotel kitchen buys onion, rice and dal by the sack, and all three have moved. Both reports name two triggers: the ₹8 a litre Nandini milk hike from 1 November, and the grocery bill.

Here is how big those grocery moves are, using the top of each old range. My maths:

  • Onion: ₹20 to ₹53 a kg. 53 ÷ 20 = 2.65, so a little over two and a half times the old price.
  • Rice: ₹52 to ₹85 a kg. 85 minus 52 = ₹33, and 33 ÷ 52 is about 63% more.
  • Toor and urad dal: ₹130 to ₹150 a kg. 150 minus 130 = ₹20, and 20 ÷ 130 is about 15% more.

The association also says some rice varieties may reach ₹100 a kg. That one is a "may", so I am leaving it out of the sums.

The onion is the loudest number. The rice is the one that hurts a meals hotel, because rice is most of the plate.

The onion has now been expensive for longer than most gym memberships stay in use.

Has anything actually been decided?

No: the DKHRA has given an estimate of 5 to 10%, and no price rise has been decided.

As of 3 November 2026, I could not find any report of a DKHRA decision, or of any hotel raising its menu after 2 November. Coastal Digest says no decision is final and the association will discuss it at its next meeting.

So a headline saying "Mangaluru food prices up 10%" is ahead of the facts. The accurate version is duller: the hotels have told us their costs and what they think that means. The boards have not moved as a group.

Are these the same prices the government reports?

Partly. On onion, the association and the government roughly agree. On rice and dal, the association's figures are higher.

The Department of Consumer Affairs publishes an all-India average retail price every day. As on 2 November 2026 it showed onion at ₹53.63 a kg, rice at ₹48.64, tur dal at ₹133.91 and urad dal at ₹126.11.

That is a national average, not Mangaluru, and it is not proof the hotels are wrong. Hotels buy particular grades of rice, and a national average blends every grade in the country. I read it as a contrast, not a refutation. But it does mean the ₹85 rice and the ₹150 dal come from one source only: the association.

Have Mangaluru hotels raised prices before this year?

Yes. On 3 May 2026, Daijiworld reported that the Mangaluru Hotel Owners' Association approved a 15% food price rise, effective immediately, after commercial LPG went up ₹993 a cylinder.

That is a different body from the DKHRA, so do not merge the two. It also means any November rise would come after earlier LPG-driven rises this year, not out of a clear sky.

What does it mean for tea, coffee and catering?

Tea or coffee already costs ₹20 to ₹25 at some hotels and ₹35 to ₹40 at others, according to the DKHRA secretary. Those are today's prices. No new rate has been announced.

Catering is the one place someone used a stronger word. A Ashwatham Hegde of the Dakshina Kannada Catering Owners Association told Coastal Digest that a 5 to 10% rise in catering rates is certain. That is the caterers speaking for caterers. It is not the hotels' estimate.

What should diners and hotels in Mangaluru do?

  • Check the menu price before you order, not at the bill.
  • Ask whether the thali or meals rate has changed. It is a normal question.
  • Expect differences between hotels. There is no common rate, so two hotels on one street can now disagree.
  • If you run a hotel and you do raise prices, change the board the same day you change the bill. Restaurants can publish a price change themselves on Plate.
  • Treat any single "prices up 10%" headline as a forecast until a hotel actually prints it.

This is the same mechanism I wrote about in what the 2026 sugar spike does to a menu: an input cost moves first, and the menu price follows later, if at all. The milk side of it is in what ₹8 more a litre of Nandini milk means, and the cup side in why coffee at some Bengaluru hotels now costs up to ₹5 more.

A plate is two numbers: what it costs and what it gives you. That is why Edible Factor shows price and calories together, where the restaurant has published them. When one of them moves, you should see it before you sit down.

A 5 to 10% estimate is a forecast, not a bill.

The onion, at least, has been consistent.
